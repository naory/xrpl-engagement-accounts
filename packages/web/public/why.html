Why Engagement Accounts

Recourse and policy-enforced escrow for AI agent payments on XRPL. Testnet reference implementation. Not for mainnet funds.

Agent payments work well when fulfillment is instant: an agent calls an API, pays per request over x402, and the exchange is finished. The model breaks once fulfillment is delayed, as with goods that ship, a job that must be delivered and checked, or a supplier that must clear customs. A push payment is final when it settles, so the buyer’s agent either pays up front and hopes, or doesn’t pay. There is no refund, no partial outcome, no delivery check, and no one to appeal to.

An engagement account is an ordinary XRPL account created for one deal and deleted when it ends. Its master key is disabled. Only a SignerList of buyer, seller, an always-on arbiter, and a policy co-signer controls it. The co-signer signs only when the deal’s policy passes, fed by signed facts from neutral attesters such as a carrier, a test suite, or a registrar. Every payout needs a quorum that the XRP Ledger enforces, not our servers. Close is one atomic Batch: it pays the parties, removes the trust line, and runs AccountDelete, returning the reserve. Nobody custodies the funds, and no single party can move them.

The right tool for instant micro-payments, and this demo keeps using it there. It settles with finality: no delivery check, no refund, no dispute. If fulfillment can fail after payment, the buyer carries all the risk.

A low-reserve, strong primitive, but all-or-nothing to one destination, and a refund waits for CancelAfter. There is no co-signer policy check before release, and a single finish or cancel condition can’t express “buyer, seller, and policy agree, or an arbiter rules” without handing one holder unilateral control.

Holding user funds brings custody, commingling, and the regulatory exposure that comes with them. Engagement accounts isolate each deal in its own ledger account, and the template validator rejects any template where a service-run signer reaches quorum alone. Neither the platform nor any single party can move the money.

Each new deal shape means custom code to write, audit, deploy, and upgrade, and every line is attack surface. Engagement accounts use only native XRPL features — SignerList multisig, master-key disable, Batch, Credentials, and AccountDelete — with no custom on-chain code. Deal logic lives in declarative templates and versioned policies the co-signer evaluates.

Card disputes give real recourse, but they were built for human consumers: slow cycles, friendly fraud, no programmable agent mandate (“this agent may pay this seller at most this amount”), and no instant stablecoin settlement. Engagement accounts keep the useful part, a neutral party who can reverse a bad outcome, and make it programmable for agents.

02 Zero-Ticket economics

Nothing is pre-signed, and no Tickets are used. Every outcome is signed live against the account’s current state, including a deadline default, so the reserve does not grow with the length of the deal or with the number of payout shapes.

Each account is pre-funded with 2.4 XRP: the reserve plus 1 XRP of headroom. Figures assume today’s network reserves (1 XRP base, 0.2 XRP per owned object), which the network sets and can change. Accounts are pre-created, so the ledger’s 256-ledger deletion wait never holds up a short deal.

  • The co-signer is the policy gate. On a normal close it signs only when the policy passes: payees, amounts, fee, and a clean close. It also keeps the deal’s timers and issues a signed notice when a default outcome falls due.
  • The arbiter handles exceptions. It rules on disputes and signs missed-deadline defaults live, after checking the facts on its own validated-ledger read. Arbiter plus one party can move funds without the other party or the co-signer. That is the same kind of trust as a card network’s dispute arbiter, stated in the open. An arbiter must hold an accreditation credential and cannot be a party to the deal.
  • Liveness is disclosed. The arbiter is on the critical path for any timed-out deal, and a default also needs one party online to sign. The buyer can confirm receipt and close early at any time.
03 Six use cases, one engine

All six use a per-deal account, a co-signer policy gate, neutral attesters, timers with default outcomes, and an arbiter. Settlement is in RLUSD.

Verified on XRPL Testnet

Marketplace escrowed purchase

marketplace-escrow@1 · flagship

A buyer’s agent buys an item from a seller. The full price is locked at open, under a signed mandate that names the seller and a maximum amount. A carrier named at open is the shipment attester, beside the policy co-signer and an accredited arbiter.

Confirm. Buyer, seller, and co-signer sign together. The seller is paid the price minus the service fee, and any surplus goes back to the buyer.

shipBy missed. No receipted shipment, so the whole balance is swept back to the buyer. The arbiter signs that default with either party.

inspectBy, no dispute. The seller is paid the price minus the fee, and any surplus is swept back to the buyer. The arbiter signs that default with either party, and the payout still goes to the seller.

Dispute. The buyer posts a dispute credential and a bond before inspectBy. The release default is not proposed. The arbiter rules, and the bond goes to the winner.

The buyer gets delivery-based recourse without trusting the seller or the platform with custody. If the buyer goes quiet after a shipment, the seller is still paid. The payout is built live from the actual balance, so stray extra funds are swept back to the buyer instead of breaking the close.

Roadmap template

SLA-backed agent jobs

A buyer’s agent hires a specialist for a dataset, a model evaluation, a code audit, or a research report. A verifier attests an artifact hash, a test or eval result, or an acceptance check. Missed delivery refunds the buyer. Passing verification, or a lapsed window, pays the provider. A disputed result goes to the arbiter. Most deals can settle with no human involved, the mandate caps the commitment, and the provider sees the funds locked before work starts.

Roadmap template

B2B trade and supplier invoices

A procurement agent locks RLUSD against a supplier order: a programmable letter of credit. A carrier’s bill of lading, customs clearance, or an inspection firm attests the documents. Funds release when those documents are attested. A missed shipment refunds the importer, and discrepancies go to the arbiter. Deal size makes the per-deal cost negligible. Depending on jurisdiction, a licensed operator may need to run the arbiter or co-signer. The design has room for those roles to be run outside the service.

Roadmap template

Metered cloud, GPU, and API commitments

Made practical by live signing

An agent pre-funds a monthly compute, GPU, or API commitment. Independent metering and uptime monitors attest usage. At period end a live close pays the provider for exact usage minus attested downtime credits and sweeps the unused balance back to the buyer. The final split is not known at open. Live signing computes it and checks the policy at close, so the buyer does not pay for unused capacity and the provider’s committed revenue stays locked.

Roadmap template

Bounties, audits, and milestones

A client funds a dev shop, an auditor, or a coding agent against a deliverable. Evidence comes from Git and CI: a merged pull request, a passing pipeline, or a signed audit report. A technical arbiter fits the dispute role. Release can be the exact amount. Staged milestones are one engagement per stage, opened together, because each account has a single signer quorum.

Roadmap template

Domains, data, and license transfers

A domain, dataset, code repository, or enterprise license changes hands over hours or days. A registrar, WHOIS or registry record, or an organization-transfer record is the proof. A missed transfer refunds the buyer. A confirmed transfer releases payment. An optional holdback is a second engagement opened at the same time. Neither side goes first on trust, and a contested transfer has a route to the arbiter.

When not to use one

  • Sub-$20 payments and instant API calls. Plain x402 is simpler and cheaper. A refundable reserve and a ~0.2 XRP close fee fit when the deal is large enough and fulfillment is delayed.
  • Subjective, in-person services with no neutral witness. If no attester can say whether the service happened, every deadline is one side’s word against the other’s. Timeouts can’t be settled cleanly, and every case ends with the arbiter.
  • On-ledger swaps or mass-market consumer sales. An atomic Batch or a DEX offer already handles a swap. Existing card protections serve thousands of small buyers better than one account per buyer.
